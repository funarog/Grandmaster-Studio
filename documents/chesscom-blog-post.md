# The Art of Modern Chess Analysis:

## The Search for Truth

To analyze a game of chess is to search for truth, but "truth" in chess has radically evolved over the last half-century. In the pre-computer era, the gold standard of chess analysis was a rigorous, collaborative, deeply human endeavor. It required a profound mixture of intuition, meticulous calculation, and psychological insight. Today, there is still plenty to learn from human insight, but we also look through the lens of modern engines. Ultimately, analysis without insight is just noise, and insight without analysis is just guesswork. The true "art" of analysis happens when human curiosity directs the power of technology to solve the right problems. This tension between human curiosity and machine capability is a modern concern across many disciplines, as mathematician Terry Tao recently explored: [A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/09/11/a-severe-misalignment-of-ai-in-mathematics/)

In his book Smyslov famously wrote:

> **The content of a game should be a search for truth, and victory a demonstration of its rightness. No fantasy, however rich, no technique, however masterly, no penetration into the psychology of the opponent, however deep, can make a chess game a work of art if these qualities do not lead to the main goal – the search for truth.**

— Vasily Smyslov
125 Selected Games Volume II: 1958–1990

## The Players

**Lajos Portisch**

Widely nicknamed "The Hungarian Botvinnik" by the Soviets, due to his disciplined, scientific, and deeply prepared approach to the openings. Portisch himself was never entirely comfortable with the label. Read his ChessBase interview. As of this writing, The Sportsman of the Nation is 89 years old and is still in good health, recently performing an aria at the 45th FIDE Chess Olympiad.

**Vasily Smyslov**

Universally celebrated in chess history for his smooth, intuitive harmony, the former World Champion viewed chess as an art form rather than a science, relying heavily on seamless piece coordination and flawless endgame technique. Sharing his personal insights in Smyslov’s Best Games Volume II: 1958-1990, he offers an invaluable, deeply subjective strategic perspective from the man who actually wielded the pieces. Former World Chess Champion Vasily Smyslov passed away on March 27, 2010, at the age of 89. To the end, though he was awed by the abilities of chess engines, he maintained that the true soul of chess remained a human endeavor. An interesting read is I am Still Needed in this World!.

### Background

The 1971 Candidates Matches served as the high-stakes knockout bracket to determine who would challenge reigning champion Boris Spassky in the famous 1972 Spassky vs. Fischer World Chess Championship. The road to the 1972 World Chess Championship began with the massive 24-player 1970 Palma de Mallorca Interzonal, where the top six finishers earned direct qualification to join seeded players Tigran Petrosian and Viktor Korchnoi in the 1971 Candidates Matches. While the rest of the matches featured tense, grueling, and heavily positional chess full of quiet draws, Bobby Fischer famously went on the most shockingly dominant run in classical chess history.

In chess, ties for reserve spots in the candidates matches were typically decided by a playoff. Portisch and Smyslov, having tied for seventh at the 1970 Palma de Mallorca Interzonal, played a six-game match that ended in a 3-3 draw. Since no candidate withdrew, the match was simply concluded rather than extended. Although effectively an exhibition, this matchup produced several fascinating games, especially this one. Smyslov employed a highly unconventional approach to the Leningrad Dutch. Portisch, usually known for his methodical opening preparation and somewhat rigid middlegame play, seemed thrown off balance. Rather than playing for a slight positional edge, he entered into complications that Smyslov appeared to assess much more accurately.

### The Game

**1. d4 f5**

Jan Timman, in his famous book, stated that as far as he was aware, Smyslov had never played the Dutch Defense. Well, Timman did not have access to a chess database. The primary method for obtaining timely chess information and game scores in 1980 was through specialized print publications, tournament bulletins, and international periodicals.

A database search will inform one that Smyslov ironically played the Dutch Defense against Mikhail Botvinnik, who was very familiar with the Dutch defense, during the 1958 World Championship. Smyslov beat Botvinnik with the defense Botvinnik was so familiar with, winning that game in 38 moves!

**Smyslov wrote**

**2. g3**

At the time, Smyslov was not concerned that Portisch might play 2.e4. Smyslov played 1. d4 f5 2. e4 (the Staunton Gambit) against Mikhail Botvinnik at the Budapest Maróczy Memorial tournament in 1952.
Smyslov was playing White, and Botvinnik, playing Black, successfully neutralized the gambit and went on to win the game in 27 moves.

Today a Dutch Defense player needs to be prepared for a possible Hopton Attack with 2.Bg5! **4. Nf3**

The engine is telling you that the most accurate way to play the position is to make slow, healthy developing moves, maintain the status quo, and simply wait.

Some say Magnus Carlsen revolutionized modern chess by weaponizing patience.

🔍 Variation: 4. c4

**4. c4 Bg7 5. Nc3 O-O 6. Nh3**

The brilliance of Taimanov’s system that Timman was referring to lies in how White develops the King’s Knight. By placing the Knight on **h3** rather than the traditional **f3** square, White accomplishes three strategic goals against the Leningrad setup:

1. **Unblocking the f-pawn:** By keeping the **f3**-square clear, White preserves the option to play an eventual **f2-f3** followed by **e2-e4**, allowing White to forcefully seize control of the absolute center.
2. **Targeting the e6-square:** From **h3**, the Knight can jump to **f4**, where it eyes the **d5** and **e6** squares, severely cramping Black’s light-squared Bishop and restricting Black’s liberating pawn breaks.
3. **Preventing tactical pins:** It takes the sting out of any potential pin or tactical counter-play Black might attempt on the long diagonal.

Timman points out that this exact system was highly popular at the time and was used effectively in the 1971 Championship of the Netherlands.

Portisch simply opted for the quieter 4.Nf3 instead.

**5... O-O 6. Bb2 d5!?**

Smyslov indicated in his writings that this move was played in the spirit of the system known in opening theory as the "Stonewall."

In Grandmaster Mauricio Flores Ríos’s book *Chess Structures – A Grandmaster Guide* (2015), he writes that the Stonewall structure arises from the Dutch Defense, the Slav, or the Catalan, with the Dutch being the most common. He notes that the Stonewall has one of the worst reputations among pawn structures, with players from all levels claiming that …​d7-d5 combined with …​f7-f5 is positionally unsound.

#### The Classical Pioneers

When Smyslov played the Stonewall against Portisch in 1971 (and when Botvinnik played it in the 1950s), they were swimming against the theoretical current. The skepticism toward the Stonewall Dutch can be traced back to highly influential classical texts, most notably Siegbert Tarrasch’s 1931 work *The Game of Chess*, where he explicitly rejected the entire Dutch Defense as unsound. This critical view was later echoed in standard opening manuals like *Modern Chess Openings (MCO)* and the *Encyclopedia of Chess Openings (ECO)*, which historically treated the Stonewall as an inferior, second-tier defense due to its static structural weaknesses.

These legendary world champions succeeded because of their profound positional understanding and their ability to outplay their opponents in the resulting complex middlegames.

#### The Modern Re-Evaluation

What makes the Stonewall "modern" today is how we understand it:
**The Human Era:** Players like Smyslov played it despite the structural weaknesses, relying on "feel" and practical chances.
**The Engine Era:** Modern neural networks (like Leela) have mathematically validated Smyslov’s intuition. They have shown that the dynamic attacking chances Black gets are fully equal to White’s static structural advantages.
In short, Smyslov was ahead of his time. He was playing "modern" chess half a century before the computers arrived to prove him right.

Timman found Smyslov’s Stonewall setup very unusual in this position, and in *The Art of Chess Analysis* continues with an in-depth study of the popular variations.

Leela’s evaluation of the variations (6…​b6, 6…​d6, 6…​a5, 6…​c6, 6…​e6, 6…​Ne4, 6…​d5) is that the win/draw/loss gap between the top and bottom is a minimal 2%, indicating most reasonable moves are playable. The position remains equal, with no single line offering a decisive advantage.

Portisch misplays the position. It is important to note that neither White nor Black is winning. So Portisch must have missed another opportunity.

Smyslov indicated earlier that 15. e5 and a center expansion was a concern to him. Well, it turns out that Portisch has a counterattack opportunity as well.

True master-level trading requires the cold-blooded willingness to accept an ugly, unconventional position if it strips your opponent of their actual playing density.

### The Consequences of 17. Bc1?

By playing 17. Bc1?, Portisch kept his bishop pair but paid a catastrophic positional toll by **Handing Over the Initiative.** Instead of forcing Smyslov to justify his forward knight leap, Portisch allowed the knight to remain a dominant outpost on c4 without giving anything up. Smyslov smoothly consolidated his position and went on to win the game.

If avoiding an exchange forces your pieces into passivity (like Bc1), the trade is almost always better than the retreat.
